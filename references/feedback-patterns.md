# Feedback patterns

What has earned and cost points so far, generalized so it applies to any company. No team's grades, names, slides or company details are recorded here, and the instructor's comments are paraphrased, not quoted.

Tags: `[feedback]` = a pattern in the instructor's own comments on CFS 1 and CFS 2 drafts; `[shared-AI]` = the composite, sanitized AI comments she shared alongside the CFS 1 rubric.

## What scored full marks `[feedback]`

- Concrete numbers that tie to the company's main risk: revenue, scale, the size of the exposure, margins and leverage against management's own targets.
- A clear, single strategic uncertainty, locked in early and carried through.
- Peers normalized for non-core items (e.g., a peer's non-core or non-operating revenue stripped out) before comparing margins.
- Pointing out that competitors face the same risk, and showing how the company is positioned relative to them.

## What scored "Partial" `[feedback]`

Every Partial was a takeaway that stopped short of a decision:

- **No trade-off.** E.g., noting high leverage without asking whether buybacks should give way to debt paydown.
- **No sensitivity.** E.g., calling the risk large without showing how a price move flows to profit.
- **No adjustment.** E.g., comparing with a peer whose numbers include non-operating gains or unrelated businesses.

## Recurring questions in margin notes `[feedback]`

- "Is this pro forma or as reported?" After an acquisition, label every year. Mixed years make growth, cost shares and debt growth look far bigger or smaller than they are.
- "Is [the risk] actually what changed the margin?" Separate what drove past results (often labor or maintenance) from the forward-looking exposure.
- "Why is this cost rising?" A deteriorating cost line deserves its driver before it is dismissed against the main thesis. Labor costs depend on bargaining agreements; say so.
- "These don't compare." When peers disclose a metric differently, reconcile it or say it can't be compared.
- The mix-shift percentages in the text didn't match the tables. Recompute them.
- Explain industry jargon on the slide or in the notes.

## Strategy questions she asks `[feedback]`

- Who will you sell to, and whose business are you taking?
- How saturated is the market (co-brand credit cards were singled out as very saturated)?
- How much will it get you, in dollars?
- Does more capacity (seats, locations, space) actually produce more revenue? Destinations, routes and locations matter.
- If you add facilities, where, and what will they earn?

She treats teams' growth ideas as worth exploring but unproven until these are answered.

## Execution rules shared after CFS 1 `[shared-AI]` `[feedback]`

1. **Focus on one uncertainty.** Don't list several macro risks; build the whole what-if around one.
2. **Quantify trade-offs.** Every strategy states its initial capital, payback period and operational trade-offs.
3. **Ensure comparability.** Adjust before computing ratios (IFRS peers, large impairments, one-time costs).
4. **Optimize visuals.** A benchmark deck she pointed to used clean summary tables, bold headers and big numeric callouts instead of dense paragraphs.

## How the shared AI comments evaluate `[shared-AI]`

- **"So what?"** Horizontal and vertical analysis is table stakes; credit comes from isolating the core operational challenge.
- **Strategic fix vs. 10-K boilerplate.** "Use AI for forecasting" or "diversify" is boilerplate; a real fix has a roadmap with KPIs, timeframes or capital trade-offs.
- **Narrative alignment.** The opening financial diagnosis should justify the final recommendation; without a central problem a deck reads as a passive accounting report, not a decision memo.
- **Polish vs. rigor.** Slick layouts can hide weak logic; placeholders (empty brackets, missing figures) get explicit deductions.
- **Rewarded:** risks framed as strategic problems that need a recommendation; specific macro risks cited from the MD&A and auditor reports; showing how financial strength can be used as a buffer.
- **Penalized:** takeaways not framed around the assignment's ask; analyzing the wrong problem; describing current successes instead of vulnerabilities; an MC slide about corporate ideals instead of a strategic message; thin recommendations; missing competitor analysis.

## Delivery `[feedback]`

- Introduce the next presenter at each handoff.
- Position monitors so you speak to the camera.
- Cut filler words by pausing.
- Watch timing.
- Tell the story of why a number matters, conversationally.

## The example deck she posted `[course]`

The instructor's CFS Project folder includes a past group's CFS 1 deck on a fast-food company as an example. These are its features, as a model for format (paraphrased):

- **Opens with the problem.** A "why are we here today?" slide that states the risk in one line and backs it with 3 headline numbers (net income, return on assets, free cash flow).
- **One-line takeaway per section.** The message from the company, the MD&A and the auditor reports each get one plain sentence before any detail.
- **Peers with their niche.** Two named competitors, each with the one strength that makes it a threat, not just a table of numbers.
- **A question as a slide title.** e.g. "Is it not cheap anymore? Will tariffs make it worse?", answered with evidence (price changes over five years).
- **Many options, then a decision.** It listed eight possible responses, then used a decision chart to compare the main ones by downstream effect (profit, demand, investor appeal) before landing on a combination approach.
- **A plain-language analogy.** One analogy explains the year's results the way you'd tell a neighbor.
- **A works-cited slide.**

Don't copy its weaknesses: it cited aggregator sites (stock-chart and market-cap websites) where the syllabus expects filings, and its options weren't priced. Later steps need dollars, timelines and filings.
